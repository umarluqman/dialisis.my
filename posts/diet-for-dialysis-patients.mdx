## Introduction

For families cooking for a dialysis patient, the most common question is: "What can they eat?" The answer matters because failing kidneys can no longer remove excess potassium, phosphate, salt and water from the body. What the patient eats every day directly affects their safety.

This guide covers the essentials, with local Malaysian food examples. It is not a substitute for advice from your dietitian - every patient's diet is different and depends on their own blood test results.

## Important: Pre-Dialysis and On-Dialysis Diets Are Not the Same

Many people get confused because kidney diet advice on the internet seems to contradict itself. The reason is simple:

- **Before dialysis (early-stage CKD):** Protein intake is usually **restricted** to slow down kidney damage.
- **After starting dialysis:** Protein needs **increase**, because some protein is lost during treatment. Dialysis patients who eat too little protein risk muscle wasting and infections.

So general kidney-health videos (including two of the videos in the references below) are more suited to prevention and early-stage CKD. In fact, Doctor Sani himself stresses in his video that the DASH diet he recommends is **not suitable for patients already on dialysis** - discuss with your doctor first.

## Potassium: The Silent Danger

Excess potassium in the blood (hyperkalemia) can cause irregular heartbeats and cardiac arrest - often without any warning signs.

**Local foods high in potassium that need to be limited:**

- Durian, banana, cempedak, jackfruit
- Coconut water and fruit juices
- Potato, tapioca, sweet potato
- Tomato, spinach, bitter gourd
- Avocado
- Soy sauce and "low-sodium" salt (most replace sodium with potassium)

**Special warning - starfruit (belimbing):** Starfruit contains a natural toxin that failing kidneys cannot remove. It can cause persistent hiccups, confusion and seizures. **Kidney patients must not eat starfruit at all**, even a small amount.

**Cooking tip:** Cut vegetables and potatoes into small pieces, soak them in water, then boil and discard the cooking water. This removes part of the potassium.

## Phosphate: The Enemy of Bones and Heart

Excess phosphate pulls calcium out of the bones and can harden blood vessels. The damage is slow but serious.

**Limit:**

- Milk, cheese, yogurt (including sweetened condensed milk)
- Anchovies (ikan bilis), cockles, liver and organ meats
- Large amounts of nuts, beans and whole grains
- Fizzy drinks like cola (they contain phosphoric acid)
- Processed meats - sausages, nuggets, smoked meat (phosphate additives)

If the doctor prescribes phosphate binders, they must be taken **with meals**, not after.

## Salt and Sodium: The Biggest Challenge in Our Cooking

Salt makes the body hold on to water, raises blood pressure, and makes patients thirsty - which makes fluid limits much harder to follow.

**Salty foods common in Malaysian cooking:**

- Belacan, budu, cencalok
- Salted fish, salted eggs
- Soy sauce, oyster sauce, instant seasonings and stock cubes
- Instant noodles, instant soups, keropok
- Processed and frozen convenience foods

**Replace with:** Ginger, onions, lemongrass, turmeric, daun kesum, pandan, lime juice. Food stays flavourful without burdening the kidneys. Studies show that cutting salt can lower blood pressure within just a few weeks.

## Fluid Limits: Not Just Drinking Water

Most hemodialysis patients are limited to around 500ml to 1 litre of fluid per day, depending on how much urine they still pass. Fluids include soup, porridge, ice, jelly and watery fruits like watermelon.

**Tips to manage thirst:**

- Suck on small ice cubes instead of drinking a full glass
- Cut down salty food (the main cause of thirst)
- Use a small cup
- Apply lip balm if the lips feel dry

Gaining more than 2-3 kg between dialysis sessions is a sign of fluid overload - it strains the heart and makes dialysis sessions more uncomfortable.

## Protein: Dialysis Patients Need More, Not Less

Every hemodialysis session removes some protein from the body. Dialysis patients are encouraged to include a good protein source **at every meal**:

- Eggs (the best and cheapest source)
- Chicken and fish
- Tofu and tempeh (in moderate amounts)

Choose leaner cuts of meat, and avoid processed meats because of their salt and phosphate content.

## Can Patients Eat During Hemodialysis?

This issue went viral when netizens questioned why dialysis centers forbid patients from eating during treatment - when a session takes 4 hours. Dr Rafidah Abdullah, a consultant nephrologist, explains in this video (in Malay):

According to Dr Rafidah's explanation, the rule is not there to make life difficult - it exists for safety reasons:

1. **Risk of a sudden drop in blood pressure.** Blood pressure already tends to fall during dialysis. When eating, blood is diverted to the digestive system - blood pressure can drop further, causing dizziness or fainting.
2. **Risk of choking.** Patients are reclined and cannot move much for 4 hours because of the access needles in their arm. If they choke or vomit, it is hard to respond quickly.
3. **Hygiene and infection control.** Removing a face mask to eat inside a dialysis unit raises infection risk, especially since dialysis patients have weaker immune systems.
4. **It interferes with fluid removal calculations.** Patients are weighed before and after each session to work out how much water to remove. Eating and drinking during treatment throws off this estimate.

Her advice is simple: **eat at home before coming, and eat again after the session ends.** Dialysis center rules exist for the patient's own safety.

## Every Patient's Diet Is Different

The guidance above covers general principles. The actual limits for potassium, phosphate, fluid and protein for each patient are set based on **monthly blood test results** - some patients have well-controlled potassium and are allowed more flexibility, others need to be stricter.

Always consult your dietitian and nephrologist before making major changes to the diet. Bring a list of the patient's favourite foods to the appointment - the dietitian can help find a safe middle ground.
