## The Short Answer: Not Necessarily

Many families panic when a doctor mentions "kidney problems" because they immediately picture a dialysis machine. In reality, kidney disease is not the same as dialysis.

According to nephrologist Prof Dr Lim Soo Kun on the Lesung Batu Medic Show, chronic kidney disease (CKD) has **five stages** — and only patients who reach **stage five** need dialysis. He estimates that millions of Malaysians have CKD, but most are in the early stages. Dialysis patients are just the "tip of the iceberg".

> **Note:** This article is for education only and is not medical advice. Every patient is different — please consult your doctor or nephrologist for treatment decisions.

## The 5 Stages of Chronic Kidney Disease

Kidney function is measured using **eGFR** — calculated from a creatinine blood test, age and gender. Prof Dr Lim explains it as the "percentage of kidney function": the higher, the better.

| Stage | eGFR | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 90 and above | Normal function, but signs of damage (e.g. protein in the urine) |
| 2 | 60–89 | Mild decline |
| 3 | 30–59 | Moderate decline |
| 4 | 15–29 | Severe decline |
| 5 | Below 15 | End stage — dialysis or a transplant may be needed |

The two main causes in Malaysia are **diabetes and high blood pressure** — according to Prof Dr Lim, 60 to 70 percent of dialysis patients in this country got there because of these two conditions.

## Acute or Chronic? An Important Difference

According to Prof Dr Lim, the first thing doctors establish is whether the problem is **acute** (just happened) or **chronic** (long-standing):

- **Acute problems** can recover if the cause is treated — for example the wrong medication, an infection, or an immune system disorder.
- **Chronic problems** cannot be fully cured, but they can be stabilised so kidney function does not decline quickly.

Nephrologist Dr Uvanesan explains the same point on the You Sihat Podcast: there is **acute dialysis**, done just once or twice for a temporary problem — that patient does not necessarily need dialysis for life. Long-term dialysis is only for patients whose kidneys have failed permanently.

## Early Stages: A Lot Can Still Be Done

This is the good news many people never hear. According to Prof Dr Lim:

- At the earliest stage — eGFR still normal but **protein leaking into the urine** — the condition can still be reversed with treatment and lifestyle changes.
- Untreated CKD can lose 5 to 10 percent of kidney function per year. With proper treatment, the decline can be slowed to 2 to 3 percent per year.
- Modern medicines like **SGLT2 inhibitors**, when started at stage 3, can delay the need for dialysis by roughly 10 years — and even longer when started earlier.

That is why specialists emphasise early screening. The tests are simple: **a urine test for protein and a blood test for creatinine**. Dr Uvanesan reminds us that early kidney disease is a "silent killer" — no pain, no symptoms. A yearly health checkup is the only way to catch it early.

## When Is Dialysis Truly Needed?

On the You Sihat Podcast, Dr Uvanesan puts it plainly: **kidney doctors themselves do not like putting patients on dialysis.** Dialysis is only recommended when there is an indication — usually at stage 5, when the kidneys can no longer:

- **Control water in the body** — fluid builds up, causing swollen legs, a puffy face, and water entering the lungs until the patient struggles to breathe
- **Control salts and minerals** — potassium that climbs too high can disturb the heart rhythm and become life-threatening
- **Produce red blood cells** — causing fatigue and paleness
- **Remove toxins** — waste products build up in the blood (uremia), causing nausea, loss of appetite, itching and confusion

Without treatment at this stage, life expectancy is very short. Dialysis is not a punishment — it is a treatment that buys time and keeps patients alive. Dr Uvanesan shares that he has patients who have been on dialysis for 10 to 20 years without major issues, provided they stay disciplined with their treatment schedule and medication.

## Options at End Stage

If the kidneys have reached end stage, there are several options — and this decision should be discussed together with family and a nephrologist:

### 1. Hemodialysis

Done at a dialysis center, usually 4 hours per session, 3 times a week.

### 2. Peritoneal Dialysis

Done by the patient at home, roughly 30 minutes per session, several times a day. More flexible for those who work or travel, and it helps preserve any remaining urine output. According to Dr Uvanesan, both types are equally effective — the choice depends on the patient's lifestyle.

### 3. Kidney Transplant

According to both Prof Dr Lim and Dr Uvanesan, this is the best treatment for end-stage kidney failure — kidney function returns to normal and quality of life is the best of all options. The challenge is finding a donor: a family donor can be arranged faster, while the waiting list for deceased donors can take many years. Dr Rafidah Abdullah's video also stresses that early referral matters — patients who come late in a weakened state may no longer be suitable for a transplant.

### 4. Conservative Care (Without Dialysis)

Some patients — especially the elderly or those with many other illnesses — choose **not** to undergo dialysis. This is a valid choice, and it is the very topic nephrologist Dr Rafidah Abdullah discusses in her video "Tak mahu Dialisis, Doktor!" ("I don't want dialysis, Doctor!"). The key points:

- The decision should be made **together with the doctor and family** (shared decision-making), not out of fear or hearsay.
- The patient needs to understand what will happen without dialysis — symptoms like breathlessness and the danger high potassium poses to the heart.
- Conservative care does not mean "no treatment" — doctors still manage symptoms with medication to keep the patient comfortable.

If you or a family member is thinking of declining dialysis, do not decide alone. Have an open conversation with a nephrologist so the decision is made with complete information.

## Conclusion

Kidney disease does not automatically mean dialysis. Most CKD patients are in the early stages, where treatment and lifestyle changes can slow — and sometimes reverse — the damage. Dialysis is only needed at stage five, and even then there are options: hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, a kidney transplant, or conservative care.

The most important thing: do not wait for symptoms. Get screened early, and discuss every major decision with your nephrologist.
