## Introduction

When doctors confirm that your loved one's kidneys have failed, the next big decision is choosing a type of dialysis: **hemodialysis (HD)** or **peritoneal dialysis (PD)**.

Both work. Both save lives. The difference lies in **where**, **how often**, and **how** the treatment is done — and which one fits better with the patient's daily life.

If you are new to dialysis, start with our basic guide: [What Is Dialysis?](/blog/what-is-dialysis). This article goes deeper — comparing the two treatments to help your family make the decision together with a nephrologist.

## How Hemodialysis Works

Hemodialysis filters blood **outside the body** using a dialysis machine, as explained in the official [MyHEALTH video by Malaysia's Ministry of Health](https://youtube.com/watch?v=WhB0mZToaw8).

The process in brief:

1. The patient undergoes minor surgery on the arm to create a **fistula** — a blood access point. The fistula needs several weeks to a few months to "mature" before it can be used.
2. At every session, two needles are inserted into the fistula — one carries blood to the machine, the other returns the filtered blood.
3. Sessions take place at a dialysis center, **3 times a week, about 4 hours per session**.

Because the schedule is fixed, HD patients need to live near a dialysis center and travel back and forth for as long as they are on treatment.

## How Peritoneal Dialysis Works

Peritoneal dialysis filters blood **inside the body**, using the lining of the abdomen (peritoneum) as a natural filter.

According to Dr Lily Mushahar, Head of Nephrology at Hospital Tuanku Ja'afar Seremban, in an AWANI Pagi segment on Astro AWANI:

- A soft tube called a **Tenckhoff catheter** is placed in the abdomen through minor surgery — **no needles, no blood leaving the body**.
- Dialysis fluid is introduced into the abdomen and left for 4 to 6 hours to absorb toxins and excess water, then drained out and replaced with fresh fluid.
- Fluid exchanges are done **3 to 4 times a day**, each taking about **20 to 30 minutes** — they fit around work, school, or daily routines.
- Everything is done **at home**. No big house needed — a clean space is enough.

### Two ways to do PD

- **CAPD (continuous, manual)** — fluid exchanges are done by hand, 3-4 times a day.
- **APD (automated)** — in an educational video by Dr Rafidah Abdullah, consultant nephrologist, a machine called a *cycler* runs several exchanges automatically **while the patient sleeps at night**. In the morning, the patient disconnects the transfer set and is free for the day.

## Comparison Table: HD vs PD

| Aspect | Hemodialysis (HD) | Peritoneal Dialysis (PD) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Dialysis center / hospital | At home |
| Frequency | 3 times a week | Every day (3-4 exchanges, or overnight with an APD machine) |
| Duration | About 4 hours per session | 20-30 minutes per exchange; the fluid works on its own in the abdomen |
| Access type | Fistula in the arm (needles every session) | Tenckhoff catheter in the abdomen (no needles) |
| Lifestyle | Fixed schedule set by the center | Flexible, self-managed schedule |
| Caregiver role | Transport to and from the center 3 times a week | Help with fluid exchanges at home (if needed) |
| Travel | Must arrange sessions at another center in advance | Easier — bring fluid supplies along |

## Pros and Cons

### Hemodialysis

**Pros:**

- Fully handled by trained nurses — the patient does nothing during the session
- Only 3 days a week; the other 4 days are treatment-free
- Regular contact with healthcare staff

**Cons:**

- Tied to the center's schedule and location
- Travel back and forth 3 times a week — a time and transport burden for patient and caregiver
- Needle insertions at every session
- Fatigue after sessions is common

### Peritoneal Dialysis

**Pros:**

- Done at home — no repeated trips to a center
- No needles
- Flexible schedule; easier to keep working or studying
- Gentler, continuous daily treatment — closer to how natural kidneys work

**Cons:**

- Must be done every day, with no days off
- Patient or caregiver needs training and discipline with hygiene
- Risk of infection of the abdominal lining (peritonitis) if hygiene steps are not followed
- Storage space needed at home for fluid supplies

## Why Malaysia's MOH Encourages PD

Dr Lily Mushahar explained on AWANI that most patients in Malaysia — around 90% — are on hemodialysis, and only around 10% choose peritoneal dialysis. The main reason is not that PD works less well, but lack of exposure and misconceptions — many worry about managing the treatment themselves at home.

In fact, countries like Thailand and Hong Kong practice a **"PD-first"** policy — peritoneal dialysis is offered as the first option. In Malaysia, Ministry of Health hospitals such as Hospital Tuanku Ja'afar Seremban also prioritize PD as the first choice for suitable patients, and the number of PD patients has been growing over recent years.

Reasons PD is encouraged:

- It eases the load on increasingly full hemodialysis centers
- Patients stay independent and productive
- It suits patients living far from a dialysis center
- The medical team visits the home and trains the patient before treatment starts

## Real Life on CAPD

In a video shared on Dr Rafidah Abdullah's channel, a patient on CAPD since 2009 shares that they still work, exercise, travel, spend time with friends — and even continued acting in theater and two films after starting treatment.

The message is simple: PD is not a barrier to living. With training and discipline, patients can stay active.

## Who Suits Which Treatment?

There is no one answer for everyone. Some general guidance from the expert discussions:

- **PD may suit** patients who are still working or studying, live far from a dialysis center, have blood vessel problems that make a fistula difficult, or want independence.
- **PD may not suit** patients who have had major abdominal surgery, or who cannot maintain treatment hygiene at home.
- **HD may suit better** patients who prefer treatment fully handled by staff, or who are not suitable for PD for medical reasons.

Also worth remembering: for eligible patients, a **kidney transplant** remains the best long-term treatment — dialysis maintains quality of life in the meantime.

## Deciding with Your Nephrologist

This decision is made **together** — doctor, patient, and family — not in a rush. Questions to bring to the appointment:

1. Does the patient's medical condition allow both options?
2. What is the patient's daily routine — work, school, caregiving?
3. How far is the nearest hemodialysis center from home?
4. Who can help at home if PD is chosen?
5. What financial support is available for each option?
6. Can the patient switch treatments later if needed?

The answers differ for every family — and that is okay. Both options are good treatments.
